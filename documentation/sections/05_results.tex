\chapter{Results on synthetic data}
\label{chap:results}

This chapter reports the quantitative performance of the three
architectures of \cref{chap:archi} on the held-out synthetic test
segment. We look at the training dynamics
(\cref{sec:res-train}), the sample-level (window-level) accuracy
(\cref{sec:res-window}), and the more operationally meaningful
event-level metrics (\cref{sec:event-eval,sec:res-event}). The chapter
closes with a cost/performance comparison (\cref{sec:res-cost}).

\section{Training dynamics}
\label{sec:res-train}

\Cref{fig:training-curves} overlays the train and validation losses
and the validation macro F1 of all three models. Several observations:

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/training_curves_comparison}
    \caption{Training dynamics. Left: train loss (dashed) and validation
    loss (solid). Right: validation macro F1 (window level). All three
    models converge within $35$ epochs and benefit from one or two
    learning-rate reductions; the residual and attention models reach
    visibly lower loss plateaus than the baseline.}
    \label{fig:training-curves}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=0.25em]
    \item The 1D-CNN converges to a higher loss plateau ($\approx 0.72$
    validation loss) than both the ResNet and the attention model
    ($\approx 0.57$). The gap between train and validation curves
    remains small for all three networks, indicating that none of them
    is severely over-fitting: the residual block and the attention
    block do extract genuinely more useful features rather than
    memorising the training set.
    \item The validation F1 starts much higher for the residual and
    attention models (above $0.71$ at the first epoch) than for the
    baseline ($0.61$). The residual variant is the fastest to converge
    to its plateau --- the skip connections seem to make optimisation
    not only easier but also faster.
    \item The attention model's curve is the most non-monotonic ---
    consistent with the higher variance of Transformer training --- but
    the \texttt{ReduceLROnPlateau} schedule cuts the learning rate
    twice and stabilises it.
\end{itemize}

\label{sec:res-window}

After training, each model's best checkpoint is loaded and evaluated on

        \texttt{fast\_ascend}      & $0.704$ & $0.737$ & $0.744$ \\
        \texttt{fast\_descend}     & $0.721$ & $0.741$ & $0.738$ \\

        \texttt{square}            & $0.703$ & $0.740$ & $0.732$ \\

        \textbf{Overall accuracy}  & $0.766$ & $0.807$ & $0.795$ \\
        \bottomrule

    ($62\,485$ windows). The residual network is the strongest on the
    anomaly classes; the attention model is slightly weaker on the
    \texttt{Normal} class.}
    \label{tab:test-window}
\end{table}

These numbers look modest --- roughly $20\%$ of windows are
misclassified --- but they are deeply misleading at the operational
level. The reason is that a window is labelled according to the
\emph{majority anomaly class} inside it, but a single anomaly typically
spans many overlapping windows that include large fractions of normal
background. Windows near the boundary of an event easily flip between
the anomaly class and \texttt{Normal} depending on whether they pass
the $10\%$ threshold. What we really care about is whether the
model identifies the event \emph{as a whole}.

\section{Event-level evaluation methodology}
\label{sec:event-eval}

We therefore evaluate the system on a continuous segment using a
\emph{detection-driven} protocol implemented in
\texttt{architectures/inference\_engine.py}.

\subsection{Continuous probability trace}

A test segment of $100\,000$ samples (immediately following the
training portion) is fed to the network through a sliding window of
size $512$ and stride $10$. Each window output produces a probability
vector of length $K=7$ which is averaged into the per-sample running
total (using $1/n_{\text{visits}}$ as weight). At the end we have a
sample-level probability matrix $\hat{P} \in \R^{100\,000 \times 7}$.

\subsection{Predicted-event extraction}

A sample is declared ``anomalous'' if $\hat{P}_{t,0} < 0.3$ (i.e.\ the
network is at least $70\%$ confident that this sample is \emph{not}
normal). Contiguous regions of anomalous samples form predicted
events; small gaps of up to $50$ samples are bridged to avoid splitting
a single event into multiple fragments.

\subsection{Class assignment for each event}

The class of a predicted event is decided by \emph{integrating} the
probability vectors over the event's duration and picking the class
with the highest cumulative score:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{y}_{\text{event}} = \argmax_{c \in \{1, \dots, K-1\}} \sum_{t \in \text{event}} \hat{P}_{t, c}.
\end{equation}
This averaging makes the classification much more robust to per-sample
noise than a majority vote on per-sample $\argmax$ predictions.

\subsection{Matching predicted to true events}

True events are extracted from the ground-truth labels by the same
contiguous-region procedure. A predicted event is matched to a true
event whenever the two intervals overlap; the match with the largest
overlap is kept. The result is split into:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=0.2em]
    \item \emph{True positives (TP)}: a true event matched to a
    predicted event with the correct class.
    \item \emph{Misclassifications}: a true event matched to a
    predicted event with the wrong class (counted as a false
    \emph{negative} for the true class).
    \item \emph{Missed events (FN)}: a true event with no overlapping
    prediction.
    \item \emph{Hallucinations (FP)}: a predicted event that does not
    overlap any true event.
\end{itemize}
Per-class precision, recall and F1 are then computed in the usual way,
and the report quotes the macro average across the six anomaly
classes.

The event-level confusion matrices for the three models are shown in
\cref{fig:confmat}.

\paragraph{Reading the numbers.} A $93\%$ event-level F1 on the
baseline is already very useful: it means that out of the $59$ events
in a $100\,000$-sample segment, the baseline correctly identifies
$55$, misclassifies $4$, and hallucinates none. The residual model
identifies all $59$ events correctly without hallucinating. The
attention model is competitive but produces two confusions and one
missed-then-misclassified event.

\paragraph{Where errors live.} The two recurring confusions are between
templates that share visual primitives:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=0.2em]
    \item \texttt{M} can look like \texttt{bell} if its second peak is
    eroded by deformation;
    \item \texttt{square} can look like \texttt{bell\_sq} if the corners
    are softened.
\end{itemize}
The residual network, with its larger receptive field and richer feature
re-use, never makes these confusions on the test segment; the baseline
and the attention model trip on a few hard examples.

\paragraph{No hallucinations.} A striking feature of all three models
on this clean test set is that the false-positive rate is essentially
zero: predicted events that do not overlap a true event are extremely
rare. We will see in \cref{chap:realdata} that this property collapses
dramatically on real, almost-pure-background data.

\section{Cost and performance trade-off}
\label{sec:res-cost}

\Cref{fig:model-cost} summarises the cost--performance trade-off across
the three architectures. Note the very small spread in training time
between ResNet and CNN+Attention (about $10$ minutes for $35$ epochs)
despite the latter having $47\%$ more parameters, reflecting the
efficiency of GPU-resident attention on short sequences ($T=65$ tokens).

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/model_cost}
    \caption{Cost--performance trade-off across the three architectures.
    Left: parameter count. Centre: total training time on a single
    RTX 5060 Laptop GPU. Right: event-level macro F1 on the synthetic
    test segment. The ResNet is, on this benchmark, the sweet spot:
    half the parameters of the attention model, a perfect F1, and
    similar wall-clock time.}
    \label{fig:model-cost}
\end{figure}
