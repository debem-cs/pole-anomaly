\chapter{Real 2015 data and the v1 noise model}
\label{chap:data}

This chapter introduces the real-world data the project is anchored to
(\cref{sec:real-data}) and the first-pass statistical model the
\emph{v1} generator was built on (\cref{sec:v1-noise-model}). The v1
generator itself --- templates, deformations, the end-to-end
algorithm --- is described in \cref{chap:v1gen}. The deeper background
re-analysis that motivated the v2 generator is deferred to
\cref{chap:reanalysis}.

\section{Real 2015 gamma dose data}
\label{sec:real-data}

\subsection{Source}

The reference recordings are four months of gamma dose measurements
collected in 2015 by an environmental surveillance beacon and provided
by the project supervisor. The dates are
\begin{center}
\texttt{24/02/2015 11:20} \quad \texttt{30/04/2015 13:10} \quad
\texttt{22/06/2015 07:31} \quad \texttt{20/10/2015 09:18},
\end{center}
each segment lasting approximately $40\,000$ minutes (about $28$ days)
at one sample per minute. The four months are stored as separate
columns in a single text file under \texttt{data-gen/data/}\,.

\begin{figure}[H]

    dispersion of a few counts and slow drifts of a few units; a small

\subsection{Statistical characterisation}
\label{sec:stat-charac}

\Cref{fig:real-overview} suggests that the signal is the superposition
of (i) a slowly-varying baseline and (ii) a high-frequency, zero-mean
fluctuation. We confirm this with a moving-average decomposition of
window length $w = 480$ samples
(\cref{fig:real-decomp}):
\begin{equation}
    b_t = \frac{1}{w} \sum_{i = -w/2}^{w/2} x_{t+i},
    \qquad r_t = x_t - b_t.
\end{equation}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{figures/real_data_decomposition}
    \caption{A $10\,000$-sample snapshot of the first month decomposed
    into a slowly-varying baseline (top, with the MA-480 baseline in
    red) and zero-mean residuals (bottom).}
    \label{fig:real-decomp}
\end{figure}

The empirical statistics, averaged across the four months, are reported
in \cref{tab:real-stats}. The baseline standard deviation is much
smaller than the high-frequency residual standard deviation, and its
slow variation is what motivates the random-walk baseline model used in
the v1 generator.

\section{v1 noise model: Gaussian fluctuations on an OU baseline}
\label{sec:v1-noise-model}

Following the empirical findings of \cref{sec:stat-charac}, v1 models
the real gamma dose as
\begin{equation}
    x_t = b_t + \eta_t,
    \quad \eta_t \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2),
    \quad b_{t+1} = b_t + \theta (\mu - b_t) + \xi_t,
    \quad \xi_t \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_{\mathrm{OU}}^2).
    \label{eq:v1-noise-model}
\end{equation}
The first equation says that the instantaneous reading is a baseline
plus zero-mean Gaussian noise; the second is the Ornstein--Uhlenbeck
(OU) discrete dynamics for the baseline, with mean-reversion strength
$\theta$ and innovation magnitude $\sigma_{\mathrm{OU}}$. With
$\theta = 4 \cdot 10^{-6}$ the baseline is very loosely tethered ---
the half-life of an excursion is roughly $\log 2 / \theta \approx
1.7 \cdot 10^{5}$ samples, much longer than our $W = 512$ analysis
windows --- which reproduces the slow drifts visible in
\cref{fig:real-overview}.

\paragraph{What v1 gets right.} The marginal distribution of
$\eta_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$ matches the empirical histogram
of the moving-average residual (\cref{fig:real-stats}, left). The OU
baseline matches the order of magnitude of the long-term drift.

\paragraph{What v1 misses.} The IID assumption for $\eta_t$ is too
strong: the empirical residual ACF in \cref{fig:real-stats} (right)
shows a smooth decay over $\sim 30$ lags rather than a sharp drop to
the $\pm 2 / \sqrt{N}$ band that white noise would produce. At the
level of the marginal histogram and the global standard deviation, the
mismatch is invisible; at the level of the local texture of a
$512$-sample window --- the input that a convolutional stem actually
sees --- it is large. \Cref{chap:reanalysis} will fix this with an
AR(1) model.
