\chapter{Signal model and synthetic data}
\label{chap:data}

No labelled real events are available, so the networks are trained and evaluated
on synthetic data. This chapter first characterises the real recordings just
enough to fix realistic parameters (\cref{sec:realdata}), then defines the
background model (\cref{sec:noise}), the anomaly morphologies
(\cref{sec:templates}) and the generator (\cref{sec:generator}). The model is
\emph{deliberately simplified}: its role is to validate the classification
approach under controlled, well-understood conditions, not to reproduce real
signals faithfully.

\section{Real data: a brief characterisation}
\label{sec:realdata}

The reference data are four months of 2015 gamma-dose recordings provided by the
supervisor (Feb, Apr, Jun, Oct), each $\approx 40{,}000$ samples at one sample
per minute. \Cref{fig:real-overview} shows a representative month. Two components
are visible: a slowly varying baseline around $\sim 99$ counts, and a
high-frequency, zero-mean fluctuation of a few counts. Separating the two with a
moving-average baseline $b_t$ and residual $r_t = x_t - b_t$ gives the per-month
statistics of \cref{tab:real-stats}: the baseline sits near $99$ counts and the
high-frequency residual has standard deviation $\sigma \approx 2.3$--$2.7$. These
few numbers are all we take from the real data.

\section{Background model}
\label{sec:noise}

Following \cref{sec:realdata}, the synthetic background is a white Gaussian
high-frequency noise on a slowly drifting baseline:
\begin{equation}
    x_t = b_t + \eta_t, \quad
    \eta_t \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2), \quad
    b_{t+1} = b_t + \theta\,(\mu - b_t) + \xi_t, \quad
    \xi_t \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\mathrm{OU}}^2),
    \label{eq:noise-model}
\end{equation}
with $\mu = 99.26$, $\sigma = 2.53$, $\theta = 4\cdot 10^{-6}$ and
$\sigma_{\mathrm{OU}} = 0.0373$ (\cref{tab:real-stats}). The first equation makes
each reading a baseline plus zero-mean Gaussian sensor noise; the second is a
discrete Ornstein--Uhlenbeck (OU) process, a mean-reverting random walk whose
very small $\theta$ keeps the baseline only loosely tethered to $\mu$, so it
drifts over hundreds of thousands of samples --- much slower than the $W=512$
analysis window. This model is intentionally crude: it captures the marginal
noise level and slow drift, but treats the high-frequency noise as independent in
time, which is the main simplification we accept at this stage
(\cref{chap:conclusion}).

\section{Anomaly morphologies}
\label{sec:templates}

We model six anomaly morphologies, giving $K=7$ classes with the background.
Each is stored as a small set of \emph{keypoints} (normalised time and amplitude)
joined by linear, exponential or smoothed half-cosine segments
(\cref{fig:templates}):
\texttt{bell} and \texttt{bell\_sq} (smooth bumps, the second with a short
plateau), \texttt{fast\_ascend} and \texttt{fast\_descend} (asymmetric ramps with
a fast edge and a slow tail), \texttt{M} (a twin-peak episode) and \texttt{square}
(a sustained plateau).

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{figures/v1_anomaly_templates}
    \caption{The six anomaly templates in normalised time and amplitude. Each is
    a short list of keypoints (markers) connected by linear, exponential or
    half-cosine segments.}
    \label{fig:templates}
\end{figure}

A template used verbatim would not generalise, so every injected anomaly is
randomly deformed before use (\cref{fig:deform}). Each keypoint is jittered by a
Gaussian of standard deviation $\nu$ (the \emph{deformation level}) under
direction constraints that preserve the qualitative shape, time monotonicity is
enforced, and the curve is then rescaled to a target amplitude and duration.
The generator samples
\begin{equation}
    \text{amplitude} \in \sigma\cdot[2.5,\,7.6], \qquad
    \text{duration} \in [200,\,800]\ \text{samples}, \qquad
    \nu \in [0.02,\,0.10].
    \label{eq:ranges}
\end{equation}
Amplitudes are expressed in multiples of the noise std $\sigma$, so the
per-sample signal-to-noise ratio of an anomaly lies in the interesting range
$[2.5,7.6]$ --- strong enough to be learnable, weak enough to be non-trivial.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{figures/v1_template_deformation}
    \caption{Five random realisations of each template under the ranges of
    \cref{eq:ranges}. The qualitative shape is preserved while amplitude,
    duration and fine shape vary.}
    \label{fig:deform}
\end{figure}

\section{The synthetic dataset}
\label{sec:generator}

The generator combines \cref{eq:noise-model,eq:ranges} into one long labelled
signal. It (i) draws a number of anomalies and their roughly evenly spaced
injection points (with $\pm 200$-sample jitter); (ii) for each, samples a
template and a $(\text{amplitude},\text{duration},\nu)$ triple and builds the
deformed curve; (iii) generates the background of \cref{eq:noise-model} in
$50{,}000$-sample chunks (so memory stays constant); and (iv) adds each anomaly
on top of the background and labels every sample with its class.
\Cref{fig:snapshots} shows two injected anomalies up close, each overlaid with
its underlying clean shape.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{figures/anomaly_snapshots}
    \caption{Two injected anomalies seen in the synthetic signal. Blue: the
    generated gamma dose (baseline $+$ Gaussian noise $+$ anomaly); orange: the
    pure anomaly shape before noise. Left: a smooth \texttt{bell}; right: an
    asymmetric \texttt{fast\_descend} (fast rise, slow decay). Both have an
    amplitude of $\sim 5$--$6\,\sigma$ above the baseline.}
    \label{fig:snapshots}
\end{figure}

The dataset used throughout this report has $N = 10^{7}$ samples with
$5{,}000$--$8{,}000$ injected anomalies, i.e.\ a density of $\sim 6$--$8$ events
per $10{,}000$ samples (about one anomaly every $1{,}500$ samples). This dense
injection is a deliberate choice for the validation benchmark: it provides
abundant, balanced examples of every morphology, at the cost of a background
prior that is far denser than a real beacon would see --- a gap we return to in
\cref{chap:conclusion}.
