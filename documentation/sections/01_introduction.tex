\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:intro}

\section{Context: continuous radiological surveillance}
\label{sec:context}

Environmental monitoring beacons measure the local radiation level by
counting the gamma photons reaching a scintillation detector in a fixed
sampling interval --- typically one minute. The resulting time series,
called the \emph{gamma dose}, reflects the natural radioactive background
of the environment, modulated by atmospheric conditions (temperature,
humidity, pressure) and, hopefully rarely, by accidental or deliberate
radiological events such as the dispersion of a radioactive plume, a
leak from a nearby facility, or the passage of a contaminated source.

The operational goal of a network of such beacons is to detect and
classify those rare events \emph{as early as possible}, so that
mitigation decisions can be taken with confidence. Three properties make
this task fundamentally harder than industrial anomaly detection in
mechanical or financial time series:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=0.2em]
    \item \textbf{High noise-to-signal ratio.} The Poisson-like sensor
    statistics produce a high-frequency dispersion that is comparable in
    magnitude to many anomalies of interest.
    \item \textbf{Slow baseline drift.} The mean radiological background
    is not stationary --- it slowly wanders with weather and seasonal
    factors, so an anomaly cannot be defined by a fixed threshold on the
    raw signal.
    \item \textbf{Class diversity.} Different physical phenomena leave
    different temporal signatures: a sudden spike (a passing source),
    a slow ramp-up (a slowly approaching plume), a sustained plateau (a
    deposited contamination), and so on. A meaningful response requires
    not just detection but classification.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Problem statement}
\label{sec:problem}

The project, supervised by Élisabeth Lahalle at the \emph{Laboratoire des
Signaux et Systèmes} (L2S, CentraleSup\'elec), formulates the problem as
a \textbf{streaming multi-class classification task} on a univariate
gamma-dose time series. Given a sliding window of recent samples, the
system must output one of seven labels:
\begin{center}
\texttt{Normal Background} ~|~ \texttt{bell} ~|~ \texttt{bell\_sq} ~|~ \texttt{fast\_ascend} ~|~ \texttt{fast\_descend} ~|~ \texttt{M} ~|~ \texttt{square}.
\end{center}

The first six anomaly classes correspond to canonical shapes identified
together with the supervisor as representative of the radiological
phenomena one might encounter on a beacon. Their precise mathematical
parameterisation is given in \cref{chap:data}.

\section{Why deep learning?}
\label{sec:why-dl}

A classical signal-processing pipeline --- baseline removal followed by
a hand-tuned shape-matching template bank --- would in principle suffice
on clean shapes, but it would scale poorly to:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=0.2em]
    \item \emph{Shape variability}: real events do not match a template
    perfectly; their amplitude, duration and time-warping vary.
    \item \emph{Noise}: at the noise levels observed in the real data,
    cross-correlation with a template becomes unreliable.
    \item \emph{Class proliferation}: adding a new event class requires
    redesigning the bank.
\end{itemize}

Deep neural networks are attractive because they learn \emph{their own}
features from data, and because the convolutional and attention building
blocks of modern architectures are very effective at recognising
patterns in 1D signals. The reference project brief\footnote{See
\texttt{biblio/projet.pdf}.} explicitly suggests evaluating
convolutional and attention layers, and we follow that guidance.

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

Concretely, this project produces:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=0.3em]
    \item A \textbf{statistical characterisation} of four months of real
    2015 gamma dose recordings, from which we extract a usable noise
    model (Gaussian high-frequency component and Ornstein--Uhlenbeck
    baseline drift, \cref{sec:real-data}).
    \item A \textbf{synthetic data generator} that injects six
    parameterised anomaly templates with controlled amplitude, duration
    and shape deformation into a ten-million-point simulated signal
    (\cref{chap:data}).
    \item Three \textbf{PyTorch architectures} of increasing complexity
    --- a baseline 1D-CNN, a residual variant, and a hybrid
    CNN+Transformer model with multi-head self-attention --- all sharing
    the same I/O contract (\cref{chap:archi}).
    \item A \textbf{training and event-level evaluation pipeline} that
    bridges the gap between sample-wise classification accuracy and
    operational event-detection metrics (\cref{chap:archi-train,chap:results}).
    \item A dedicated \textbf{robustness study} that sweeps background
    noise and shape deformation independently and reports the
    F1-degradation curves of all three architectures
    (\cref{chap:robustness}).
    \item Application of the trained models to real, unlabelled 2015
    data, together with a careful \textbf{diagnostic analysis} of where
    and why they fail in the real world, leading to concrete
    recommendations (\cref{chap:realdata}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Report organisation}
\label{sec:org}

The report is laid out in six parts. \cref{chap:theory} provides the
theoretical background --- convolutions, residual learning, attention
mechanisms and the relevant training machinery --- with enough
mathematical detail to make the architectures self-contained for a
reader without a deep-learning background. \cref{chap:data} describes
the real data and the synthetic generator built from it.
\cref{chap:archi,chap:archi-train} present the three neural networks
and how they are trained. \cref{chap:results,chap:robustness} report
the quantitative results, first on a clean test segment then under
controlled stress. \cref{chap:realdata} discusses the qualitative
behaviour on real recordings and the open questions that remain.
\cref{chap:conclusion} concludes and lists the next steps that are
on the table for the project's next iteration.

All code, data generation scripts and figure-generation utilities
necessary to reproduce the contents of this report are included in the
project repository.
