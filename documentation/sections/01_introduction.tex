\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:intro}

\section{Context: continuous radiological surveillance}
\label{sec:context}

Environmental monitoring beacons measure the local radiation level by
counting the gamma photons reaching a scintillation detector in a fixed
sampling interval --- typically one minute. The resulting time series,
called the \emph{gamma dose}, reflects the natural radioactive
background, modulated by atmospheric conditions (temperature, humidity,
pressure) and, hopefully rarely, by accidental or deliberate
radiological events: dispersal of a radioactive plume, a leak from a
nearby facility, the passage of a contaminated source.

The operational goal of a network of such beacons is to detect and
classify those rare events \emph{as early as possible} so that
mitigation decisions can be taken with confidence. Three properties
make this task fundamentally harder than industrial anomaly detection
in mechanical or financial time series:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=0.2em]
    \item \textbf{High noise-to-signal ratio.} The sensor statistics
    produce a high-frequency dispersion that is comparable in magnitude
    to many anomalies of interest.
    \item \textbf{Slow baseline drift.} The mean radiological background
    is not stationary --- it wanders slowly with weather and seasonal
    factors, so an anomaly cannot be defined by a fixed threshold on
    the raw signal.
    \item \textbf{Class diversity.} Different physical phenomena leave
    different temporal signatures: a sudden spike (a passing source),
    a slow ramp-up (an approaching plume), a sustained plateau (a
    deposited contamination), and so on. A meaningful response requires
    not just detection but classification.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Problem statement}
\label{sec:problem}

The project, supervised by Élisabeth Lahalle at the \emph{Laboratoire
des Signaux et Systèmes} (L2S, CentraleSup\'elec), formulates the
problem as a \textbf{streaming multi-class classification task} on a
univariate gamma-dose time series. Given a sliding window of recent
samples, the system must output one class label per window. The label
set evolved between the two iterations of the project:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
v1 (seven classes) & \texttt{Normal} ~|~ \texttt{bell} ~|~ \texttt{bell\_sq} ~|~
                     \texttt{fast\_ascend} ~|~ \texttt{fast\_descend} ~|~
                     \texttt{M} ~|~ \texttt{square} \\[0.4em]
v2 (four classes)  & \texttt{Normal} ~|~ \texttt{bell} ~|~
                     \texttt{fast\_ascend} ~|~ \texttt{fast\_descend}
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The reasons for shrinking the class set in v2 will become clear in
\cref{chap:reanalysis,chap:v2gen}: only the three retained shapes
actually appear in the seventeen plausible events that we
hand-labelled on the real recordings.

\section{Why deep learning?}
\label{sec:why-dl}

A classical signal-processing pipeline --- baseline removal followed by
a hand-tuned shape-matching template bank --- would in principle
suffice on clean shapes, but it scales poorly to:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=0.2em]
    \item \emph{Shape variability}: real events do not match a template
    perfectly; their amplitude, duration and time-warping vary.
    \item \emph{Noise}: at the noise levels observed in the real data,
    cross-correlation with a template becomes unreliable.
    \item \emph{Class proliferation}: adding a new event class requires
    redesigning the bank.
\end{itemize}

Deep neural networks learn their own features from data, and the
convolutional and attention building blocks of modern architectures
are very effective at recognising local and global patterns in 1D
signals. The reference project brief\footnote{See
\texttt{biblio/projet.pdf}.} explicitly suggests evaluating
convolutional and attention layers, which we did.

\section{The two iterations}
\label{sec:two-iters}

This report covers two iterations of the same end-to-end pipeline.

\paragraph{Iteration 1 (v1).} A noise model fitted on the four months
of 2015 data --- IID Gaussian high-frequency component on an
Ornstein--Uhlenbeck baseline --- and six hand-designed anomaly
templates, injected at a density of $\sim 7$ events per $10\text{k}$
samples into a $10^{7}$-sample synthetic signal. Three architectures
of increasing complexity (1D-CNN, ResNet 1D, CNN+Attention) were
trained on this corpus. On the synthetic test set the system worked
very well: all three architectures cleared a $90\%$ event-level macro
F1, the residual network reached $100\%$. But on the real recordings
the system failed dramatically, in three architecture-specific ways.

\paragraph{Iteration 2 (v2).} A re-analysis of the real background
revealed that the high-frequency residual is autocorrelated rather than
white, well described by an AR(1) process with $\varphi \approx 0.86$.
A round of hand-labelling identified $17$ plausible events across the
four months, from which we measured empirical event amplitudes
(log-uniform $[15, 40]$ counts), durations (log-uniform $[30, 1000]$
samples), and a template breakdown (only \texttt{bell},
\texttt{fast\_ascend}, \texttt{fast\_descend} appear). The generator
was re-tuned accordingly --- AR(1) noise, sparser injection
($\sim 1.5$ events per $10\text{k}$ samples), three templates, absolute
amplitude/duration scales --- and the same three architectures were
re-trained on the new corpus. On the real recordings, the v2 models
sit on the \texttt{Normal Background} class for the quiet stretches and
fire only where a visible event is present.

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

Concretely, this project produced:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=0.2em]
    \item A \textbf{statistical characterisation} of four months of
    real 2015 gamma-dose recordings, in two passes of increasing depth:
    a v1 fit of an IID-Gaussian-plus-OU model, and a v2 reanalysis that
    establishes the AR(1) structure of the high-frequency residual
    (\cref{chap:data,chap:reanalysis}).
    \item A \textbf{set of seventeen hand labels} of plausible real
    events, with empirical amplitudes, durations and shape assignments
    (\cref{sec:reanalysis-labels}).
    \item Two generations of a \textbf{synthetic dataset generator}: v1
    (white noise, six templates, dense injection) and v2 (AR(1) noise,
    three templates, sparse injection, calibrated against the labels).
    Both produce a $10^{7}$-sample labelled signal in constant memory
    (\cref{chap:v1gen,chap:v2gen}).
    \item Three \textbf{PyTorch architectures} of increasing complexity
    --- a baseline 1D-CNN ($\sim 23\text{k}$ params), a residual network
    ($\sim 75\text{k}$), and a hybrid CNN+Transformer with multi-head
    self-attention ($\sim 110\text{k}$) --- all sharing the same I/O
    contract (\cref{chap:archi}).
    \item A \textbf{training and event-level evaluation pipeline}
    bridging sample-wise classification accuracy and operational
    event-detection metrics (\cref{chap:archi}).
    \item Quantitative \textbf{synthetic-test results} for v1 and v2,
    and a \textbf{robustness study} that sweeps background noise and
    shape deformation independently for both (\cref{chap:v1results,%
    chap:results,chap:robustness}).
    \item Application of both v1 and v2 models to the real 2015 data,
    with a careful \textbf{diagnostic analysis} of where v1 fails and
    how v2 closes the gap qualitatively
    (\cref{chap:v1real,chap:v2real}).
\end{enumerate}

\label{sec:org}

convolutions, residual learning, attention mechanisms, and the relevant
training machinery --- with enough mathematical detail to make the
architectures self-contained for a reader without a deep-learning
background. \Cref{chap:data} describes the real 2015 recordings and

\cref{chap:v1results,chap:v1real} the v1 synthetic-test results and
the real-data failure that motivated v2. \Cref{chap:reanalysis} is the

v1. \Cref{chap:v2gen} documents the v2 generator. \Cref{chap:results,%
chap:robustness,chap:v2real} report the v2 results: training,
event-level evaluation, robustness sweeps, real-data inference.
\Cref{chap:conclusion} concludes.
